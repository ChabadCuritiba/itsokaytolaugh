Aviva on Happiness: No Complaining, No Sorrow

Two rules. Harder than they sound, simpler than you think, and older than you’d guess.

5 min readLife & people

When I asked Aviva what her secret to happiness was, I expected something complicated. A routine, a philosophy, a book recommendation. Instead she gave me two rules, short enough to fit on a sticky note:

No complaining. No sorrow.

That’s it. I laughed — and then I spent the next several weeks failing at both.

Rule one: No complaining

Try it for a day. Not a week, a day. You’ll discover that complaining isn’t something you do occasionally. It’s background noise. The weather, the traffic, the line, the service, the price, the government, your knee.

Complaining feels harmless, even honest. But every complaint is a small rehearsal of what’s wrong. Say it enough and you become an expert in it.

The Torah noticed this a long time ago. In the desert — fed by manna, guided by clouds of glory — the people became misonenimמתאונניםComplainers — those who seek pretexts to grumble (Numbers 11:1)., complainers, looking for something to grumble about (Numbers 11:1). Surrounded by open miracles, they still found the one thing that wasn’t quite right.

If they could do it in the wilderness of miracles, we can certainly do it in a Tuesday afternoon.

A complaint is a request that hasn’t been brave enough to ask yet.

Here’s what Aviva’s rule doesn’t mean: it doesn’t mean pretending everything is fine. If something is broken, fix it. If someone hurt you, talk to them. A complaint becomes useful the moment it turns into a request — or a thank-you for what is working.

Rule two: No sorrow

This one sounds harsher. No sorrow? What about loss, grief, real pain?

Chassidus makes a distinction that helped me understand it. There is atzvusעצבותHeavy, paralyzing sadness — the kind that shuts a person down (Tanya ch. 26, 31). — heavy, sinking sadness that makes you smaller, stuck, and passive. And there is merirusמרירותBitterness — an honest, active ache about what’s wrong that moves a person to change (Tanya ch. 31). — an honest, bitter ache over what’s wrong that wakes you up and pushes you to act.

The first is a dead end. The second is a doorway. The Tanya teaches that a person should avoid the first and allow the second — and even then, the bitterness is meant to lead back to joy.

So “no sorrow” isn’t “no tears.” It’s “don’t move in.” Feel what you feel, let it move you, then get up. Sorrow is a place you pass through, not an address.

Sorrow is a place you pass through, not an address.

Why two rules are enough

What I love about Aviva’s rules is that neither one tells you to feel happy. They only tell you what to stop feeding. Stop feeding complaints. Stop feeding heaviness. And then, quietly, joy has room to come back — because joy was never really gone. It was just crowded out.

That’s the whole secret. Not adding something new, but clearing space for what was there all along.

I still fail at both rules. Often. But now when I catch myself mid-complaint, I hear her voice, and I usually start laughing. Which, I suspect, was the point.

  2. Tanya, chapters 26 and 31 — on atzvus (sadness) versus merirus (bitterness).
