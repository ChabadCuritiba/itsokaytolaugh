Why Even the Temple Times Weren’t Perfect Joy

At the very height of Jerusalem’s glory, the joy still came with an asterisk. That’s the good news.

7 min readTorah & mystery

When we imagine the happiest moment in Jewish history, most of us picture the same scene: Jerusalem, the Beis HaMikdashבית המקדשThe Holy Temple in Jerusalem. standing in its glory, crowds of pilgrims streaming up for the festival, music everywhere. If perfect joy ever existed after Eden, surely it was there.

And it really was spectacular. The Mishnah describes the celebration on Sukkos with something close to awe:

“Whoever has not seen the rejoicing of the Simchas Beis HaShoeivah has never seen joy in his life.”Mishnah, Sukkah 5:1

Simchas Beis HaShoeivahשמחת בית השואבה“The Rejoicing of the Water-Drawing” — the all-night Sukkos celebration in the Temple courtyard. — the Rejoicing of the Water-Drawing. Torches so bright that every courtyard in Jerusalem was lit. Sages juggling torches. Levites singing on the fifteen steps. All night long.

So why would anyone say that wasn’t perfect joy?

The first asterisk: something was missing

The Talmud lists five things that were present in the First Temple but absent from the Second: the Ark, the heavenly fire, the open Divine Presence, the spirit of prophecy, and the Urim v’Tumim (Yoma 21b). The Second Temple was holy, magnificent — and noticeably incomplete.

The First Temple had everything in place, and yet its era is filled with prophets pleading, warning, rebuking. The books of Isaiah and Jeremiah are not the soundtrack of a people living in uninterrupted bliss. Even at the peak, the cracks were showing.

Even at the very peak, the cracks were showing.

The second asterisk: joy had an address

Here’s a subtler point. In Temple times, the greatest joy was located. It happened in a particular city, in a particular courtyard, on particular days. You had to travel to it. Then the festival ended, and you went home.

That’s beautiful — but it’s also a limit. Joy you have to travel to is joy you can leave. It’s seasonal. It visits.

The prophets describe something else entirely for the future:

“The redeemed of G‑d shall return and come to Zion with song, and everlasting joy upon their heads.”Isaiah 35:10

Simchas olamשמחת עולם“Everlasting joy” — joy that doesn’t end or depend on time and place. — everlasting joy. Not a festival you attend, but a crown you wear. Not a place you visit, but the way the whole world feels.

The third asterisk: “then”

And there’s the verse we keep coming back to on this site: “Azאז“Then” — referring to the time of the Redemption. — then our mouths will be filled with laughter” (Psalms 126:2). The Talmud reads this as teaching that complete, mouth-filling laughter belongs to the time of the GeulahגאולהThe Redemption — the era of Moshiach. (Berachos 31a).

Then. Not in the desert. Not in King Solomon’s court. Not even in the Temple courtyard by torchlight. Then.

The Temple wasn’t the destination. It was the preview.

Why this is good news

At first this can feel deflating. If even that wasn’t perfect joy, what hope do we have?

But flip it around. It means the best is genuinely ahead of us, not behind us. Judaism isn’t nostalgic for a golden age we’ll never see again. It’s anticipating a joy greater than anything that has ever existed — greater than the Water-Drawing, greater than Solomon’s Temple.

And it means our joy now isn’t a cheap imitation. Every time we laugh in galut, we’re tasting a sample of something that has never been fully served. The Temple was a preview. Our laughter is a preview. The feature presentation is still coming.

Whoever has not seen the rejoicing of the Water-Drawing has never seen joy. True. But nobody — not yet — has seen what’s next.
