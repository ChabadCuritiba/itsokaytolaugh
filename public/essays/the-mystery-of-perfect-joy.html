The Mystery of Perfect Joy

We spend the word “joy” like small change. The real thing hasn’t been seen since Eden — and it’s on its way back.

9 min readTorah & mystery

Ask a hundred people what they want out of life and most will say some version of the same thing: I just want to be happy. Then ask them what happiness is. Watch the pause. We chase it, schedule it, buy it in small doses — and almost none of us can define it.

That pause is where this whole project began. Because the more you look at joy, the less it looks like a feeling and the more it looks like a mystery. Not a puzzle you solve once, but a mystery you live inside of, like love, or time, or G‑d.

A garden called Delight

The Torah doesn’t open with a lecture on happiness. It opens with a place. The first human beings live in Gan Edenגן עדןThe Garden of Eden. Eden means “delight” or “pleasure.” — and eden, in Hebrew, means delight. Humanity’s first address was, quite literally, the Garden of Delight.

What made it so? Not the fruit or the weather. In Eden, the good was obvious. There was no static between a person and their Creator, no gap between what is and what ought to be. Joy wasn’t something you had to work up to. It was the air.

Then came the sin, and with it something new entered the world: mixture. Good and evil tangled together. And immediately the Torah introduces a new word into the human vocabulary:

“With itzavon shall you eat of it all the days of your life.”Genesis 3:17

ItzavonעצבוןToil, pain — from the root etzev, the same root as atzvus, sadness. — toil, pain, sorrow. It comes from the same root as atzvus, sadness. The first consequence of leaving the Garden of Delight wasn’t just hard work. It was the arrival of heaviness itself.

Humanity’s first address was, quite literally, the Garden of Delight.

Why it’s so hard now

We’ve been living outside the garden ever since. Our tradition has a name for this condition: galutגלותExile. Not only a place, but a state of concealment where the good is hidden., exile. And galut isn’t only geographic. It’s a state of mind in which the good is hidden and the problems are loud.

You can hear galut in how we talk. We are fluent — native speakers, really — in the language of tzoresצרותYiddish (from Hebrew tzaros): troubles, woes, aggravation.. Ask someone how they are and you’ll get a weather report of complaints. The news is built on it. Social media runs on it. We bond over what’s wrong.

None of this makes us bad people. It makes us people in exile. In galut, misery feels realistic and joy feels naïve. Laughter seems like a distraction from the serious business of suffering.

But here is where the tradition does something surprising.

Joy as a commandment

King David doesn’t say “serve G‑d, and if you happen to feel good, wonderful.” He says:

“Serve G‑d with joy; come before Him with song.”Psalms 100:2

And the Torah, in the middle of its most frightening chapter, explains that hardship comes “because you did not serve the L‑rd your G‑d with joy and gladness of heart” (Deuteronomy 28:47). Not because you didn’t serve — because you served without joy.

That is an astonishing claim. It means joy isn’t a bonus for good behavior. It’s part of the behavior. It’s something you can practice, strengthen, and choose — even in exile, even when the good is hidden.

Chassidus adds that simchaשמחהJoy, gladness. In Chassidic thought, a force that “breaks through boundaries.” poretz geder — joy breaks through boundaries. Sadness narrows a person; joy widens them past their own limits. Joy is how people in a small, concealed world start acting bigger than their circumstances.

Joy isn’t a bonus for good behavior. It’s part of the behavior.

Then — and only then

So is perfect joy available now, if we just try hard enough? Here the Talmud says something that sounds almost like a buzzkill. Rabbi Shimon bar Yochai taught that a person shouldn’t fill their mouth with laughter in this world, because the verse says:

“Then our mouths will be filled with laughter, and our tongues with song.”Psalms 126:2 · Talmud, Berachos 31a

Azאז“Then.” The verse places full laughter in the time of redemption. — then. When? When G‑d returns the exiles to Zion. The verse places full, mouth-filling laughter in the time of the GeulahגאולהThe Redemption — the era of Moshiach, when the world’s hidden goodness is revealed., the Redemption.

This isn’t a ban on laughing. It’s a statement about scale. Our laughter now is real, but partial. It’s a mouthful, not a full mouth. Something is still missing, and every honest person feels it — that faint ache under even the best day.

And that ache is the clue. You can only miss something you were made for.

So what do we do with that?

We laugh anyway. Not to escape the exile, but because joy is the most accurate response to what’s actually true: that the world is good at its core, that the concealment is temporary, and that the story ends with mouths full of laughter.

Every time we choose joy in galut, we’re doing more than coping. We’re rehearsing. We’re practicing the native language of a world that hasn’t fully arrived yet — which, it turns out, is exactly how it arrives.

Our laughter now is a rehearsal for a world that isn’t here yet — which is exactly how it gets here.

Joy is one of the deepest mysteries of creation. We haven’t fully tasted it since Eden. We won’t fully taste it until the Geulah. And in between, we have this: the strange, serious, holy work of laughing on purpose.

It’s okay to laugh. Seriously.
