Don’t Let Miserable People Bring You Down

Misery is contagious. So is joy. You get to decide which one you catch — and which one you spread.

6 min readLife & people

You walk into a room in a perfectly good mood. Ten minutes later you feel heavy, irritated, a little hopeless — and nothing actually happened to you. Someone in that room was miserable, and you caught it like a cold.

We all know this person. Sometimes we are this person. And the question of what to do about it turns out to be one of the most practical questions in the whole study of joy.

Happiness is a serious problem

Years ago, the writer and radio host Dennis Prager wrote a book with a title that stopped me cold: Happiness Is a Serious Problem. His argument, roughly, is that happiness isn’t only a personal preference. It’s closer to a moral obligation — because how we feel doesn’t stay inside us. It leaks out onto our spouses, our kids, our coworkers, the stranger at the checkout.

An unhappy person, he points out, rarely makes only themselves unhappy. So working on your own joy isn’t selfish. It might be one of the kindest things you can do for the people around you.

Working on your own joy isn’t selfish. It’s a gift to everyone who has to live near you.

The flip side nobody mentions

If that’s true — if moods are contagious — then the reverse is also true. Other people’s misery isn’t only their problem. It becomes yours the moment you let it in.

And here’s the hard part: miserable people are often very persuasive. Misery sounds realistic. It sounds smart. A cynic always seems like the most sophisticated person in the room, and a person full of joy can seem a little naïve. That’s the culture of galutגלותExile — a state where the good is hidden and complaints feel like realism. talking: in exile, gloom passes for wisdom.

But it isn’t wisdom. It’s just gloom with good vocabulary.

Compassion is not contagion

Let’s be careful here. There’s a difference between a person who is suffering and a person who is miserable. The first is in pain and needs you. The second has made pain into a worldview and wants company.

You can sit with someone in grief without adopting their despair. You can listen to someone’s tzoresצרותYiddish: troubles, woes. without signing up to carry them home. Real compassion actually requires that you stay a little lighter than the person you’re helping — you can’t pull someone out of a pit by climbing in and sitting down.

Five ways to stay sunny

  1. Name it. Just noticing “oh, I’m catching someone else’s mood” breaks half the spell.
  2. Don’t debate a mood. You will not argue a miserable person into joy. Arguing only feeds it. Change the subject, change the room, or change the energy.
  3. Bring your own weather. Decide how you want to feel before you walk in. A small private intention — a verse, a song, a thank-you — works like an umbrella.
  4. Limit the dose. Some people you love, and you’ll see them anyway. Some feeds, channels, and group chats you can simply mute. That isn’t cold. It’s hygiene.
  5. Be the contagious one. The best defense is offense. Joy is catching too — and Chassidus teaches that a little light pushes away a great deal of darkness. Be the person whose mood other people catch.

Joy is a responsibility

The Torah tells us to “serve G‑d with simchaשמחהJoy, gladness..” It doesn’t add “unless the people around you are grumpy.” Joy is ours to guard. Nobody can take it from us unless we hand it over.

So the next time you feel yourself sinking in someone else’s gray cloud, remember: you’re allowed to stay dry. You’re allowed to be the sun in the room. Seriously.
